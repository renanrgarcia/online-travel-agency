Airline retailing & agentic AI · briefing

What you need to know before you join the flights team

A travel company wants artificial intelligence (AI) agents to shop several airlines' Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) and pick the best option. That sentence hides three separate problems — content sourcing, ranking, and orchestration — and only one of them is actually an AI problem. This is the map.

00

The short answer

Keep the large language model (LLM) out of the price path. Search, fan-out, dedupe, ranking, pricing and ticketing stay deterministic .NET. The model handles intent at the front door and explanation at the back — everywhere a human would have used words instead of numbers.

If you take one position into the first meeting, take that one. It is the difference between a demo that impresses for a week and a system that survives contact with a real look-to-book ratio. Everything else in this document is the supporting argument.

Five claims you should be able to defend

  1. "AI compares the airlines" is the wrong framing. Ranking flights is a scoring function over structured offers — price, duration, stops, layover quality, fare rules, ancillary cost, carrier reliability. That is a deterministic function you can test, tune, and explain. Google does exactly this. An LLM asked to rank fifty offers is slower, costs money per request, and can silently reorder results between two identical queries.
  2. Your tech lead is right about n8n, but for a sharper reason than he gave. n8n scales horizontally in queue mode. The real mismatch is that flight shopping is a latency-critical transaction path with a high rate of queries per second (QPS) that suppliers meter per request, and n8n is a workflow engine built for low-QPS business processes. It belongs in the back office — supplier onboarding, refund approvals, reconciliation, alerting — never in the shopping path.
  3. For .NET on Azure, the aligned choice is Microsoft Agent Framework. It went 1.0 in April 2026, with a Durable Task extension for stateful, resumable orchestration. The Claude Agent SDK — Anthropic's software development kit (SDK) for building agents — that you pitched is real and good, but it ships for TypeScript and Python only — it is the wrong shape for their core services, though a fine choice for internal tooling.
  4. Content strategy is the actual hard problem, and it is commercial, not technical. Whether you can even see a fare depends on contracts, accreditation from the International Air Transport Association (IATA) or the Airlines Reporting Corporation (ARC), and negotiated look-to-book limits. The "paid licenses" your lead mentioned are the centre of gravity of the whole project.
  5. Look-to-book is the constraint that shapes the architecture. Suppliers meter shopping requests. An agent that "explores options" by firing extra searches will breach the ratio, get throttled, and cost real money. Every agentic design has to carry a search budget.
On your wife's question

Yes — Google Flights does most of this already, and studying it is the fastest way to understand what "best option" means in this domain. But it is a metasearch product: it ranks and hands off. What your lead is describing is an online travel agency (OTA): it ranks, sells, takes payment, issues the ticket, and owns the customer when the flight is cancelled. 01

How a flight actually gets sold

You cannot design the AI layer until you can draw this chain. Every architectural constraint later in the document falls out of it.

The vocabulary shift you need to know

The industry is midway through moving from Passenger Name Record (PNR)-and-ticket to offer-and-order. Under the old model the airline publishes fares into a shared pool, a global distribution system (GDS) holds the booking record — the PNR itself — and a separate e-ticket document represents the entitlement. Under the New Distribution Capability (NDC) and the offer/order model the airline constructs a personalised, time-limited offer for you specifically, and if you accept it becomes an order — one object covering the seat, the bag, the seat selection and the meal.

This matters for AI design more than it looks. An offer is priced for a context and it expires. You cannot cache it the way you cached a published fare, and you cannot let an agent reason for forty seconds over an offer that lives for ninety. IATA has replaced the old four-level NDC certification with the Airline Retailing Maturity (ARM) index, and more than seventy airlines now carry an ARM rating — so "does this carrier support NDC" is no longer yes/no, it is a capability profile you have to model per carrier.

Interview-grade detail

When someone says "the price changed at checkout", the cause is almost always one of three things: a cached offer that expired, an availability change between shopping and pricing, or a currency or tax recalculation at the fulfilment step. Knowing that distinction on day one will make you look like you have shipped travel before.

The strategy that actually gets chosen

Almost nobody picks one. The pattern that wins is a hybrid, sequenced by risk:

  1. Start with an API-first aggregator to get a working end-to-end booking flow in weeks, including ticketing you do not have to be accredited to do.
  2. Add a GDS once volume justifies the contract and the accreditation work, because per-booking economics improve sharply at scale.
  3. Add direct NDC for your top three to five carriers in your core market, where the margin and ancillary access pay for a bespoke integration.

Design the supplier layer for this from day one. One ISupplierConnector interface, one canonical offer model, per-supplier adapters behind it. If the team has hard-coded a single supplier's response shape into the domain model, that is the first thing worth fixing and a very good thing to find in week one.

Where MCP fits

There are Amadeus Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers in the community, Sabre has published one, and Travelport is betting publicly on MCP as the integration layer for AI travel booking. Useful to know, and genuinely useful for internal tooling and prototypes. But MCP is a tool-calling protocol for models — it does not replace your production supplier adapters, which need connection pooling, circuit breakers, retry semantics and per-supplier rate accounting that a generic MCP server will not give you.

03

The four constraints that kill naive designs

1 · Look-to-book

The ratio of shopping requests to actual bookings. Airlines and GDSs earn on bookings and pay for searches, so they meter you. Industry estimates put the ratio in the tens of thousands to one and describe it as unsustainable. Sabre's answer is cache-powered shopping that predicts when to poll live versus serve validated cache, reporting up to a 28% reduction in look-to-book with ~95% accuracy against live offers and sub-second responses.

The trap

The most natural agentic design — "let the agent try a few date variations and nearby airports to find something better" — multiplies your shopping calls by five to twenty per user. That is a contractual and financial problem before it is a technical one. Any exploration an agent does must run against your cache and your historical data, not against live supplier calls.

2 · The latency budget

Users abandon flight search fast, and every millisecond you spend is one you took from the slowest supplier's p95 (95th-percentile latency). Here is a realistic budget for a search that must show first results inside three seconds.

Intent parse (LLM)0.3–0.8s
Supplier fan-out (p95)1.5–5.0s
Normalise + dedupe~50ms
Deterministic ranking~100ms
Explanations (LLM)0.4–1.2s

The red segment is the one that must not block. Stream ranked results to React the moment ranking finishes, then fill in explanations asynchronously as they arrive. If an LLM call sits in the critical path of the results page, you have spent a third of your budget on prose.

3 · Price integrity

A displayed price is a promise. Regulators treat it as one, and so do customers. This gives you an absolute rule that shapes the whole AI layer: a language model may never author a number that reaches the user. Prices, taxes, baggage allowances, fare rules and change fees are passed through from validated supplier fields. The model may reference them, reorder the prose around them, and translate them — it may not compute or restate them from memory.

Enforce it structurally, not by prompt discipline. Give the model tool outputs containing opaque tokens ({"price_ref":"OFF_8812"}) and render the real values from your own store on the way out. A prompt that says "do not invent prices" is not a control; a rendering layer that cannot emit a model-authored number is.

4 · Fulfilment is not the same as booking

Selecting an offer, creating an order, taking payment, and issuing the ticket are four distinct operations with four distinct failure modes, and they can fail independently after the customer has already been charged. Every one of them needs an idempotency key, a durable state machine, and a compensating action. This is exactly the workload Durable Functions exists for — and exactly the workload you should never put behind a stateless prompt loop.

The ranking question, answered properly

Your tech lead's actual ask — "the agents should evaluate which are the best options" — resolves to a weighted scoring function over normalised offers. Something in this shape:

Total price, including ancillaries the traveller will actually buyw₁
Total elapsed journey timew₂

Layover quality — duration, terminal change, connection riskw₄

Start with hand-tuned weights, ship, then learn them from booking outcomes. Publicly circulated estimates of Google's "Best" tab put price around 40%, duration 30%, stops 20% and layover quality 10% — treat that as a sanity check on your starting point, not gospel.

Note w₈. A metasearch engine has no margin term. You do, and that makes your ranking a commercial instrument as well as a user experience (UX) one. Keep that weight visible, versioned and auditable — and keep it inside the deterministic scorer where product and legal can see it, which is another reason it must never live inside a prompt.

The framing that lands in the meeting

The model does not choose the flight. The model explains the choice, in the traveller's language, with their stated priorities in mind. That is the part humans are bad at and scoring functions cannot do — and it is genuinely worth building.

06

A reference design you can defend

The parts worth arguing for

  • A canonical offer model. One internal representation every supplier maps onto. Version it. This is the single highest-leverage thing in the codebase.
  • Budget accounting in the supplier layer. Every shopping call is counted against a per-supplier, per-tenant budget, and the counter is a first-class metric next to latency. Without it you will find out about your look-to-book problem from your account manager.
  • Two caches, deliberately different. An offer cache with a short, offer-aware TTL for supplier responses; a semantic cache for LLM outputs, where repeated and near-identical prompts are common and published results put savings in the 60–85% range.
  • Durable sagas for anything past "add to cart". Payment authorised, order created, ticket issued, confirmation sent — each checkpointed, each compensable.
  • Token spend as a monitored metric. Same dashboard as latency and error rate. If nobody owns the number, it grows.

What to steal from Google Flights

  • The "Best" tab itself. Two tabs — Best and Cheapest — is an honest user interface (UI). It tells the user a judgement was made and lets them opt out of it. Copy this exactly.
  • Explaining the trade-off inline. "€40 more, three hours shorter" is the whole product. This is the one place an LLM adds something the scorer cannot.
  • Calendar and price-graph views. These are what a user actually wants when they say "flexible dates" — and critically, they are served from cached, pre-computed data, not from live shopping calls. That is the design that keeps look-to-book survivable.
  • Speed as the feature. QPX's whole reputation is evaluating enormous numbers of itinerary combinations fast. You will not match it, but the lesson holds: the differentiator in flight search is latency and coverage, not cleverness.

What to steal from Expedia

  • The merchant model buys you the bundle. Owning payment end to end is also what lets Expedia sell flight and hotel as one transaction instead of two — real margin, and exactly the PCI/chargeback exposure Section 10 walks through. The two arrive together; there is no version of the bundle without the compliance surface.
  • The loyalty math is the honest number on where flights sit. Expedia's unified One Key program pays 2% back in rewards currency on lodging and 0.2% on flights — a ten-to-one gap. That is this table's "commercial ranking incentive" row, quantified: flights are the product that gets someone into the funnel, not the one that pays for the funnel. If your company sells more than flights, that ratio is worth having in your head before you argue for headcount on the flights side specifically.
  • The fourth product can be an API for someone else's storefront. Expedia for Business now licenses its search-and-book stack to over 60,000 partners — close to 40% of 2025 revenue. Etraveli built an entire company on the same instinct (Section 02, and the "also sells access" row above). A content-and-ranking stack good enough to sell to travellers is often good enough to sell to other travel companies, and that second buyer usually has a much shorter sales cycle than a consumer one.
  • Romie answers a different question than this document does. Section 04 assumes the intent-parsing agent's input is a search box. Expedia's Romie takes it from a Short Message Service (SMS) group chat or an email thread instead. Worth five minutes if the input surface for your own intent agent is still an open question rather than a given.

What to steal from Decolar/Despegar

  • The hybrid model is the honest starting point for a Brazilian OTA. Mixing merchant and agency lets you take the margin where your own accreditation and cash position support it, and take the commission where they do not, rather than betting the whole business on one model on day one.
  • SOFIA plus sponsored listings is a second revenue lever, not just a chat feature. Pairing an AI travel assistant with ads embedded directly in ranked results means the margin term in Section 04's scorer, w₈, may eventually need a "sponsored" flag alongside it — a commercial decision to make deliberately, in the open, not something that creeps in.
  • Payment rails are part of the product here, not an afterthought. Pix and interest-free instalments (parcelamento) are what a Brazilian traveller expects at checkout the way a saved card is expected elsewhere — see Section 10 for what that changes architecturally.
  • The acquisition and the pivot that followed it are the same instinct as Etraveli and Expedia, a third time. Prosus paid $1.7B for Despegar in 2025, and Despegar responded by licensing its own booking-and-payment stack as white-label infrastructure to banks. "Sell the stack, not just the storefront" (Section 07) is not a one-off; it is what mature travel-tech platforms tend to do once the core product is good enough.
The cautionary tale in your own market

Grupo 123 — 123milhas and MaxMilhas — has been in recuperação judicial (Brazil's version of Chapter 11) since August 2023, around R$2.4 billion in claims, after its "Promo" flash-sale product sold tickets faster than it could actually source them and then cancelled them en masse. As of February 2026 the case is still working through a creditor settlement proposed to stretch payments across nearly seven years. 123milhas has since suspended Promo but keeps selling regular tickets; MaxMilhas trades normally. The lesson is not "avoid discounts" — it is that a pricing or inventory promise your supplier layer cannot actually back is a business-model failure long before it is a technical one, and it is exactly the kind of promise an under-constrained agent could make on your behalf if nobody puts a hard boundary between "the model found a great deal" and "the deal is real."

Airbnb doesn't sell flights — three things are still worth stealing

Airbnb is a two-sided lodging and experiences marketplace, not a travel agency, and none of the GDS, NDC or PNR machinery this document is about applies to it. But it runs the sharpest ranking-and-trust engineering in consumer travel, and three specific things transfer even though the business does not.

  • It bets the opposite way on ranking, on purpose — and both bets are correct. Airbnb's search reportedly re-ranks every result per searcher across 800-plus signals, with no fixed position for any listing, rather than the single deterministic scorer Section 04 argues for. Airbnb has deep repeat-behaviour signal on most searchers and comparatively low per-item regulatory stakes. A flight search has thin repeat signal on most travellers — most people book a handful of times a year — and a hard requirement, Regulation 1008/2008 in Section 10, to show a defensible, explainable price and rank. Personalised black-box ranking is the right call when you have the data density and the regulatory room for it. A flights platform has neither, yet.
  • Its February 2026 pricing incident is the clearest case study available for the price-integrity rule. A defect in Smart Pricing, Airbnb's automatic host-pricing algorithm, reportedly decoupled it from real demand and overrode hosts' own price ceilings — one widely reported case had an Austin host's calendar for South by Southwest (SXSW) week reset from $350 to $149 a night with no manual step in between. Airbnb's stated position was that it bore no financial liability for the resulting host losses. That is Section 03's rule — a system may never author a number that reaches a stakeholder unchecked — playing out on the supply side instead of the demand side, with a real, reported dollar cost attached. The lesson generalises past language models: any automated pricing system, facing travellers or facing suppliers, needs the same structural bound-checking. A policy that assumes the algorithm behaves is not a control, any more than a prompt is.
  • It is the most public data point on what "AI-native" costs to build. Airbnb reports roughly 60% of new code now written with AI co-authorship and says it shipped 100 features in one quarter in 2026, including a review-highlights feature that synthesises over a billion guest and host reviews per listing. Whatever your team decides about Microsoft Agent Framework, this is the scale of process change other consumer travel companies are already treating as ordinary — useful context for how fast "the AI feature" can turn into "how we build everything."

What does not transfer

Google does not issue your tickets, does not answer the phone when Lufthansa cancels, and does not carry your merchant risk. Roughly half of an OTA's engineering effort lives after the booking button — order servicing, schedule-change handling, refunds, exchanges, involuntary re-accommodation. When your lead says "use Booking.com as inspiration", the honest reading is: inspiration for the storefront. The machinery behind Booking.com's flight storefront is a wholesale partner they chose not to build — Expedia chose to build the equivalent itself, which is the real difference between those two OTAs and a large part of why their margins and their engineering headcounts do not look alike.

Worth raising early

"Do we want to be a metasearch, an OTA on top of a wholesale partner, an OTA that builds its own merchant machinery the way Expedia did, or a hybrid merchant-and-agency model like Decolar's?" is a strategy question disguised as an architecture question, and the answer changes the content strategy, the compliance surface, and the size of the team. If it has not been settled, asking it will be more valuable than any code you write in month one.

The unit economics that constrain the design

Do this arithmetic before you design anything. It is what turns "should the agent be more agentic?" from an opinion into a number. Assume a modest margin per ticket and a 2% session-to-booking conversion.

And that is before supplier search costs. At a published rate of $0.005 per search past a 1500:1 ratio, an agent that fires ten extra exploratory searches per session adds $2.50 per booking on its own. The two effects compound, and they compound in exactly the scenario your tech lead is worried about: scale.

The control that makes this safe

Give every session a hard budget — n model turns and m supplier shopping calls, enforced in the supplier layer and the agent host, not in the prompt. When a session exhausts its budget it degrades to the deterministic path rather than failing. Then put both counters on the same dashboard as p95 latency. Cost discipline you can see is cost discipline that holds.

Semantic caching is the other lever and it is a large one — published production results cluster around 60–85% cost reduction, because a meaningful share of queries in any real workload are semantically near-identical. "Cheap flights to Lisbon in December" arrives a thousand times a day in a thousand phrasings.

How this breaks in production

Ask these in design review. Each one is a real incident that has happened to a real OTA.

Failure modes, ordered by how quietly they fail
Failure How it shows up Control
Model states
a wrong fare		 Silently. A confident, plausible, incorrect baggage allowance in a summary. Regulatory and trust exposure. Structural: models emit references, the render layer resolves real values.
Offer expired
at checkout		 Conversion drop with no error in the logs — users just leave. Offer-aware TTL, re-price before payment, show the delta honestly.
Duplicate booking
on retry		 Two tickets, one customer, a refund and an apology. Idempotency keys on every mutating supplier call, end to end.
One slow supplier

breach		 Not in your telemetry at all — in an email from the supplier, or a throttle. Budget counters as first-class metrics with alerting well below the contractual limit.

10

Compliance, in the amount you need to be useful

You do not need to be the compliance expert. You need to know enough to not design something that has to be rebuilt.

EU 1008/2008
The European Union (EU)'s Regulation 1008/2008 requires the final price to be shown at all times, with air fare and all unavoidable, foreseeable taxes and charges displayed separately from the first time a price appears. Optional extras must be opt-in and communicated clearly at the start of booking. This constrains your results page, not just your checkout.
US DOT
The United States Department of Transportation (DOT) requires ancillary fee transparency: carriers, ticket agents and online travel agencies (OTAs) must disclose baggage and change/cancel fees wherever fare and schedule information is shown. Note that fare-advertising flexibility rules were being revisited during 2026 — worth checking the current position for whichever markets you sell in.
PCI DSS
The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) applies if you are merchant of record — you own card-data scope. Design so raw card data never touches your services: hosted fields or a Payment Service Provider (PSP)-tokenised flow. Decide this before anyone writes a payment endpoint.
SCA / 3DS
Strong Customer Authentication (SCA), typically implemented via 3-D Secure (3DS), adds a step that fails, times out, and drops conversion. It has to be a state in your booking saga, not an afterthought.
GDPR / LGPD
The EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and Brazil's Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados (LGPD) treat passenger names, dates of birth and passport details as personal data, with passport data especially sensitive. This directly limits what may go into a prompt or a log. Redact before the model, not after.
EU AI Act
A ranking-and-recommendation system for consumer purchases is not high-risk, but transparency obligations around AI interaction apply. Practical impact: say when a user is talking to an AI, and keep the ranking logic explainable. Both are things you would want anyway.
IATA / ARC
Accreditation determines whether you may issue tickets yourself or must fulfil through a consolidator or a managed-content provider. This is a commercial gate, and it sets your time-to-market more than any technical decision will.
ANAC 400/2016
Resolução da Agência Nacional de Aviação Civil (ANAC) nº 400/2016 requires the total price — airfare plus airport tariffs and government-owed amounts — to be shown at every stage of the search, not just at final checkout: the same "no bait-and-switch" instinct as EU 1008/2008 above. Only government-mandated taxes may be broken out separately from the headline total.
Pix / parcelamento
Pix, Brazil's instant-payment rail, has no chargeback mechanism — a Pix payment is irrevocable, which moves fraud and refund risk onto your own policy instead of the card network's dispute process. Interest-free instalments (parcelamento) are a default expectation at Brazilian travel checkout, not an upsell; they create a working-capital gap, since Billing and Settlement Plan (BSP) Brazil wants paying within five business days while the traveller pays over months, commonly bridged with a Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL) partner or receivables factoring.
The one that bites AI projects

Passenger data in prompts. It is the easiest mistake to make and the hardest to walk back once it is in a vendor's logs. Establish the redaction boundary in week one, before anyone has built a habit around passing a whole booking object into a model.

11

Thirty days to credible

Ordered so that each week produces something you can show or say. Roughly ten focused hours a week.

Week 1 — the domain

  • Get a Duffel test-mode access token — free, no credit card, self-serve in about a minute. Run a flight-offers search against Duffel Airways, their sandbox carrier, then a price call, then a booking in test. Feel the shape of an offer/order response and how much it differs from what you expected. (Amadeus Self-Service, the traditional first stop here, had its self-serve portal decommissioned in July 2026 — Duffel test mode is the closest free equivalent today.)
  • Read one carrier's NDC offer response end to end. Notice how much of it is not the price.
  • Sketch the distribution chain from section 01 from memory. If you can draw it on a whiteboard, you can hold your own in the meeting.

Week 2 — the constraints

  • Build a two-supplier fan-out in .NET with per-supplier timeouts, a circuit breaker and partial results. This is the real skeleton of the whole product.
  • Add a canonical offer model and map both suppliers onto it. Deliberately pick two with different schemas.
  • Add a shopping-call counter and expose it as a metric. Now you have a look-to-book budget.

Week 3 — the AI layer

  • Microsoft Agent Framework in a .NET project: one intent agent producing a typed search request, validated against a schema before it reaches the supplier layer.
  • One explanation agent that receives ranked offers as structured references and produces prose without authoring a single number. Prove the boundary holds by feeding it a deliberately wrong price reference.
  • Add semantic caching on the intent agent and measure the hit rate on your own repeated queries.

Week 4 — the production shape

  • Wire a Durable Functions saga for the booking flow with an idempotency key and one compensating action. Kill the process mid-flow and watch it resume.
  • Write a one-page ranking spec with explicit weights, and a test that proves ranking is deterministic across identical inputs.
  • Write the deck: distribution chain, AI surface map, framework recommendation, cost model. Four slides. You now have a point of view rather than a set of opinions.
The demo that wins the room

Natural-language query in, streamed ranked results out in under three seconds, explanations filling in asynchronously afterwards, and a counter in the corner showing supplier calls and token spend for that session. The counter is the part that gets you hired — it says you understand that this system has to be affordable, not just clever.

What to ask before you write any code

Roughly in order of how much each answer changes the design.

Are we metasearch, OTA, or OTA on a wholesale partner?Determines whether we own ticketing, payment and disruption — and therefore half the scope.
Which content sources are contracted today, and at which tier?Self-Service and Enterprise are different products with different limits. "We use Amadeus" is not an answer.
Are we IATA/ARC accredited, or fulfilling through a consolidator?Gates whether we can issue tickets at all.
What is our contractual look-to-book limit, and what happens if we breach it?This is the hard budget every agentic design has to fit inside. Ask for the number.
What is the actual margin per ticket?Sets the AI cost ceiling. Section 08 is unanswerable without it.
Business-to-consumer (B2C), business-to-business (B2B) agent-facing, or corporate travel?Corporate means policy engines and approval chains. B2B means a different interface and different latency tolerance.
Is there a ranking service today, or is it sort-by-price?If ranking already exists, the AI work is explanation. If not, that scorer is the first real deliverable.
Are we merchant of record, and where does PCI scope sit?Decides the payment architecture before anyone writes an endpoint.
What is p95 search latency today, and what is the target?Sets the entire AI latency budget.
Do we retain historical search and booking data?Needed for learned ranking weights, cache heuristics and price-prediction features. If it does not exist, start collecting it now — the data debt compounds.
What is already in Azure — API Management (APIM), Service Bus, Container Apps or Azure Kubernetes Service (AKS), Redis, Structured Query Language (SQL) Database or Cosmos DB?The agent layer should land in the existing platform, not beside it.
Is there a Foundry or Azure OpenAI deployment already, in which region, with what quota?Region affects both latency and data residency.
Who is the buyer of the AI feature, and what metric moves?Conversion uplift, agent productivity, or ops cost — three different first sprints. Nothing else prioritises the backlog as sharply.

Glossary

GDS
Global Distribution System. The shared marketplace connecting airlines and sellers. Amadeus, Sabre and Travelport handle the overwhelming majority of GDS bookings between them.
NDC
New Distribution Capability. IATA's XML standard letting airlines distribute rich, personalised offers directly rather than through published fares.
ARM index
Airline Retailing Maturity. Replaced the old four-level NDC certification with a capability profile. 70+ airlines certified.
Offer / Order
The modern model. An offer is a time-limited, contextual price; an order is the accepted offer and everything attached to it. Replaces the PNR-plus-e-ticket split.
PNR
Passenger Name Record. The reservation record. Not the ticket — the ticket is a separate document.
Look-to-book
Shopping requests divided by bookings. The industry's conversion metric, and your cost driver.
LCC
Low-cost carrier. Frequently absent from GDS content; usually reached by direct connect or an aggregator.
Virtual interlining
Stitching two unconnected tickets into one itinerary. Cheap, and the connection risk is yours, not the airline's.
Fare basis / fare rules
The code and the conditions attached to a fare — changes, refunds, baggage, stay requirements. Where most edge cases live.
Ancillaries
Bags, seats, meals, priority. Increasingly where the margin actually is.
Consolidator
An accredited party that issues tickets on your behalf when you are not accredited yourself.
MCP
Model Context Protocol. Standard interface between models and tools. Being adopted across travel — Sabre, Travelport and Amadeus all have public positions on it.
A2A / AP2 / UCP
Emerging protocols for agent-to-agent communication, agent-initiated payments, and agent commerce. Early, but moving fast in travel specifically.
